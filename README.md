# 2020-SolarWinds-Supply-Chain-Attack-Post-Incident-Written-Report
This post-incident report evaluates the nation-state supply chain attack targeting SolarWinds' Orion network monitoring software. It details how foreign intelligence threat actors breached internal build environments, injected the SUNBURST backdoor into digitally signed updates, and compromised approximately 18,000 client organizations—including U.S. federal agencies and critical enterprise sectors. The analysis documents key financial impacts, forensic discovery timelines via FireEye, root causes involving build-pipeline security gaps, and recommended defense-in-depth frameworks including Zero Trust, strict least privilege, and Data Loss Prevention (DLP) controls.

**Executive Summary**  
The SolarWinds attack of 2020, is one of the most significant and recent case of data breach on high-profile targets and organizations. The attack was a Russian state sponsored supply chain attack that took significant expertise and funding that compromised SolarWinds’ Orion network monitoring software which was used as a vector in their espionage and exfiltration operation. Through SolarWinds, the attackers gained inner network access into thousands of SolarWinds’ clients, including thousands of high-profile organizations and United State’s federal agencies, and resulted in significant revenue loss by its clients over $12 million in damages. The consequences of this attack further signify the importance of cybersecurity and taking proper diligence in regular audits for organizations. Overall, the supply chain attack is now a valuable case study with many lessons to learn from, both the precautions and actions to take to prevent similar incidents in scale from occurring again.  
**Incident Description**  
The SolarWinds Cyber Attack occurred between the September of 2019 to March 26, of 2020 and the attack was conducted by Russian Foreign Intelligence that focused on the third-party network monitoring service provider SolarWinds (Fortinet, n.d.) (GAO, 2021). This attack resulted from hackers accessing the SolarWinds network, and injecting their malicious code into Orion. Orion is a SolarWinds software monitoring suite that is used by its clients (GAO, 2021). The attackers made several injections into SolarWinds’ software updates, first to test and then their malicious code named SUNBURST that resulted in clients installing the trojan code that compromised their networks (Fortinet, n.d.). The malicious code gave attackers access to thousands of client’s network information, and eventually backdoor access into company’s network and devices (GAO, 2021). SolarWinds offer a network monitoring software, and has thousands of customers globally including the United States federal government, and it is estimated that 18,000 clients were affected by this attack (GAO, 2021). The hackers primarily focused on exploiting and spying on the data of “high-value targets, including the federal government” (GAO, 2021). Clients of SolarWinds suffered an average of 11% loss of their annual revenue, with US client entities suffering the highest financial loss of 14% loss of annual revenue on average, UK client entities suffered a loss of 8.62% of their annual revenue on average and Singapore client entities suffered a loss of 9.08% of their annual revenue on average (IronNet, 2021, p. 8). Additionally, the attack primarily focused on clients in the technology sector, accounting for 13.16% of affected clients whom also suffered the biggest impact, while the rest of the affected sectors included finance, utilities, and public services (IronNet, 2021, p.8). In total, the average dollar loss of SolarWinds’ clients is around $12,341,607 (IronNet, 2021, p.8).  
**Root Cause Analysis**

This critical attack started because the attacker group was able to breach into SolarWinds’ networks without them noticing. Once the attackers were inside of the SolarWinds’ networks, they created and tested code injection into the networks, a few months later, the group started to roll out malicious code into the update files (GAO, 2021). One of the key factors the attackers used in evading detection was that they were able to have their injected files digitally signed by SolarWinds effectively disguising it as trusted files, this then paved a way for the attackers to use the compromised Orion software and SolarWinds’ servers to create a backdoor into client networks (Vaughan-Nichols, 2021). Once the attackers gained backdoor access into client target networks, they were able to install additional malware in order to steal or spy on the target’s data (Fortinet, n.d.). Client data was especially vulnerable due to the fact that Orion is a software used to monitor network performance and had access to “system logs generated by client IT assets” (Fortinet, n.d.). The attack has been reviewed to be extremely sophisticated and resource intensive and was eventually recognized to be carried out by the Russian Foreign Intelligence Service, which suggests that their primary motive was a nation state espionage campaign (GAO, 2021).

**Timeline of Events**

The attack first started in September of 2019 when the attackers breached SolarWinds’ internal networks, then in October there were traces of attempts to integrate malicious code into the provider’s infrastructure allowing for “remote exploitation” of SolarWinds’ software (GAO, 2021). In February of 2020, the threat actors injected the malicious code into SolarWinds’ Orion software, and in March, SolarWinds started to roll out updates that were compromised by the trojan code disguised by SolarWinds digital signatures (GAO, 2021). The malware continued to be active and distributed throughout 2020, and was first identified in November of 2020 by FireEye, a cybersecurity service firm and began investigation and informed SolarWinds of their compromised software on December 12, of 2020 (GAO, 2021). Between that time, Microsoft also released a statement that some of their cloud servers were compromised and informed federal agencies that sensitive data may have been breached, and started to respond to the attack (GAO, 2021). Similarly, SolarWinds released security patches between December 15 and 16, 2020 that prevented Orion from the possibility to further spread malware (Fortinet, n.d.) (Khan, 2021). On December 13, 2020 to December 16, 2020, the United States Department of Homeland Security’s (DHS) Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) took immediate action in mitigating further exploitation of federal assets, and coordinated a nationwide response to the attack which involved the FBI, CISA and NSA (GAO, 2021). From December 18, 2020, to February 18, 2021, SolarWinds, Microsoft, and CrowdStrike, continued forensics, analysis, and reporting of how the breach occurred, the extent of the infection and impact (GAO, 2021). Finally, from February 23 to February 26, 2021, SolarWinds, Microsoft, and CrowdStrike made senate and house appearing for the security oversight (GAO, 2021). Later analysis revealed that Orion was primarily compromised between March, 2020 to June 2020 (Fortinet, n.d.).   
**Lessons Learned**  
SolarWinds took quick action to mitigate and find a solution to the damages, once they were notified of a breach (Fortinet, n.d.). The third-party provider released patches as soon as possible that disabled Orion from possibly spreading more malware and restricted its level of access to its client’s internal networks (Fortinet, n.d.). As a result, Orion has been updated to monitor client networks without putting client IT environments at risk (Fortinet, n.d.). On top of finding a solution to the problem, SolarWinds and partners also took action to notify its clients to take steps on their end as well to investigate and put a stop on any further damage (Khan, 2021). It is also crucial for organizations to have active Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) systems in place to review logs for suspicious activity (Fortinet, n.d.). This is where FireEye, a cybersecurity services firm played the part in detecting an intrusion in their systems through logs and identified that it is a supply chain attack and its source (Khan, 2021). In this incident, the attackers had backdoor access into SolarWinds’ network and tools for multiple months without detection. Even upon malicious code injection and rollout, their actions were still undetected by SolarWinds which led to significant damage to its clients (Fortinet, n.d.). This suggests that SolarWinds may need to improve their auditing, review, and cybersecurity of their own networks, platform, and software (Khan, 2021). Moreover, SolarWinds likely needed to emphasize their penetration testing efforts as well, as breaches to their systems can lead to detrimental consequences for their clients (Fortinet, n.d.).

**Recommendations**  
Moving forward, this incident has increased the need and awareness for organizations to implement a zero-trust model to their networks, especially for third-party providers or contractors (Khan, 2021). It is also wise for organizations to uphold strict principle of least privilege guidelines to ensure access by entities within and outside the organization only has access to what they need and nothing more (Khan, 2021). Additionally, organizations that use third-party services that border or access potentially sensitive data or the organization’s inner networks, should be investigated for additional risk that may be introduced from their products (Fortinet, n.d.). Organizations and third-party providers should host strict and regular auditing procedures for their networks and systems, as such may detect an incident earlier on and prevent excess damage (Fortinet, n.d.). Another recommendation according to Fortinet suggests organizations to implement and utilize a data loss prevention (DLP) system, which can also help in the detection and prevention of exfiltration attempts.   
**Conclusion**  
In conclusion, the SolarWinds attack of 2020 was a major attack that now serves as an important case study for the security of organizations small and large. More specifically, the incident was a supply-chain attack that compromised SolarWinds’ network monitoring software, Orion. The attackers were a nation-state sponsored group and injected their malicious code named SUNBURST into Orion, infecting client networks through SolarWinds’ compromised update patches. The impact of the attack caused massive loss of revenue from thousands of clients and even affected the United States' national security. Overall, the breach was swiftly handled once noticed, but not before going unnoticed for months. This serves as a reminder for the importance of cybersecurity, security management, and regular and careful auditing procedures, which could have lessened the extent of the damage.
